What This Week's Most Watched AI Chart Doesn't Show: Cheap Models Are Earned

We ran the experiment the token panic is missing. It cost $1.82.

Wall Street needed 48 hours to crown a new favorite chart. On June 9, macro strategist Andreas Steno Larsen told his followers that the Silicon Data LLM Expenditure Index was the only chart worth watching, and that if token pricing rolled over, the hardware trade was finished for the cycle. On June 10, Citadel Securities built a research note around the index's decline. By June 11, JPMorgan was telling clients the panic was a speed bump.

Every camp is arguing about whether the AI trade survives the rollover. Nobody is asking the question that decides which way the chart moves next: who actually gets to use cheap models. The uncomfortable answer, for most teams, is not whoever wants to.

The most watched chart in AI measures two things at once

The chart Wall Street is trading on cannot distinguish a price war from a behavior change. The Silicon Data LLM Expenditure Index is an expenditure weighted average of what the entire market pays per million tokens, irrespective of model. Silicon Data itself published a correction this week saying the index should really have been called a price index, because what it captures is the market's marginal willingness to pay for intelligence.

That subtlety matters because two completely different events move the index the same direction. When DeepSeek cuts prices 75 percent and Xiaomi's MiMo lands at roughly 99 percent below frontier rates, the index falls with zero change in anyone's behavior. When teams migrate workloads from frontier models to cheap ones, the index also falls. Goldman's One Delta desk reads the current decline as the first story, a price war. Citadel reads it as the second, substitution toward cheaper models. The chart cannot tell them apart, and Silicon Data says so on its own index page.

The index's history this year is a behavior diary. It fell early as users moved en masse onto cheap open weight models, then climbed sharply as the same users moved en masse back to frontier closed models. Silicon Data described that climb in four words: everyone is Claude-maxxing. Since early June the index has stalled and rolled over, which is the move that launched a thousand research notes.

Here is what the four camps share. Steno reads the rollover as a bear trigger for memory and data centers. JPMorgan dismisses it as the smallest speed bump on the way to higher spending. Citadel reads substitution and a bifurcation between frontier and everyday usage. Coinbase's CEO predicts 80 percent of workloads migrate to models that cost 99 percent less within 18 months. Bear or bull, every one of these readings treats model choice like a consumer choice, a thing you decide the way you switch grocery brands. You are about to see why that assumption is wrong, and why the right frame changes what the chart predicts.

Not one of the famous token bills is a pricing story

The incidents everyone cites as evidence that AI is too expensive are actually evidence that nobody built a harness. Walk through the public record of the past month and map each disaster to what was missing.

An AI consultant told Axios that one corporate client spent a reported 500 million dollars on Claude in a single month, because no usage limits existed for employees. That is not a price problem. That is a missing constraint layer, the part of the stack that decides what an agent or an employee is allowed to spend before the invoice arrives.

Amazon reportedly removed an internal token leaderboard after engineers gamed it, a practice the industry now calls tokenmaxxing. A leaderboard that counts tokens with no verification denominator measures activity, not output. The moment tokens became the metric, they stopped measuring anything, which is Goodhart's law executed at company scale.

Uber's CTO told The Information in mid April that "the budget I thought I would need is blown away already," and Citadel's back of the envelope on that remark is brutal: a budget exhausted 28.5 percent of the way through the year implies spending at roughly 3.5 times the planned run rate. Microsoft cancelled its Claude Code subscriptions and moved to its own CLI on cost grounds. GitHub Copilot's shift to token based billing produced bills up to 19 times higher for some users. In every case the spend was unmetered, unattributed, or invisible until a billing change exposed it.

The market read these stories as AI is too expensive. The correct reading is these organizations had no layer between the model and the money. The two readings predict opposite futures, because prices fall on their own and harnesses do not build themselves.

So we measured what the premium actually buys

We ran the experiment that the entire discourse is missing, and it cost one dollar and eighty two cents. The question: on the kind of work that dominates daily API traffic, what does frontier pricing buy you? We took 40 small tasks of the most common kind, 20 structured extractions (invoices, bank statements, logs, itineraries) and 20 well specified functions (parsers, validators, interval math), and ran each against two models from the same family: Claude Fable 5, the frontier model Anthropic launched on June 9 at 10 dollars per million input tokens and 50 per million output, and Claude Haiku 4.5 at 1 and 5, a tenth of the price.

The instrument has two strictly separated layers, and this design is the whole experiment. The harness sees only what a real production team could deploy: JSON schema validation, domain invariants like line items summing to the stated total, and the visible test suite. Ground truth, meaning gold values and hidden edge case tests, is used only for scoring and is never shown to any model. An output that passes the production checks but fails ground truth is an uncaught error, the kind that ships.

Each task ran in two conditions. Single shot: send the prompt, ship the answer. Harness: run the production checks, and on failure retry once with the verifier's feedback. Temperature zero where the API allows it (Fable 5 deprecates the parameter, so it ran at provider defaults). The 40 tasks split into a primary set and a held out set fixed before any results existed.

The result: single shot, both models scored 98 percent, identical. With the harness, both reached 100 percent, identical. Cost per correct outcome: 0.0019 dollars for Haiku with the harness against 0.0221 for Fable 5, which means the frontier premium was an 11.6x surcharge for zero additional correctness on this class of work. With 40 of 40 correct, the statistical floor on that 100 percent sits near 91 percent, so treat this as an existence proof on a small sample, not a universal law. Every task, transcript, and line of harness code is in the repo for you to re run, for about the price of a coffee refill.

The only invisible error came from the expensive model

Across 160 runs, exactly one output passed every production check while being wrong, and the frontier model produced it. Fable 5's query string parser passed all four visible tests and failed the hidden edge cases, the precise failure mode that ships to production and becomes an incident three weeks later. Haiku's only miss, an anagram grouping bug, was caught immediately by the visible tests and fixed on the retry for a fraction of a cent. The cheap model's error was visible and free to fix. The expensive model's error was the invisible kind.

One error proves nothing statistically. It still says the thing no pricing sheet will: the insurance premium does not include the inspection.

The experiment delivered a second finding we did not plan. Across both runs there were three other disagreements between model output and our gold answers, and on audit, all three were defects in our own specifications. One gold paraphrased where the source was verbatim, one read a label field differently than any reasonable person would, one disagreed about a trailing period. The models out specified the benchmark authors three times out of three.

Sit with that, because it is the real lesson about verification layers. The hard part of verifying AI output is not running checks. It is stating what correct means precisely enough to check. That specification work is exactly the layer teams skip when they pay frontier prices as a substitute, and it is the layer that made a 10x cheaper model safe to run in this experiment.

The market splits on verifiability, not difficulty

Armstrong's 80/20 prediction has the right shape and the wrong axis. The Coinbase CEO frames the coming split as easy work going cheap while hard work stays frontier. Look instead at what actually migrated first in the real market: code generation and structured extraction, which are not the easiest workloads. They are the most verifiable ones. Code compiles or it does not. Tests pass or they do not. A schema validates or it does not.

The rule hiding in the data: work migrates to cheap models when its output can be checked mechanically, and stays on frontier models when verification itself requires frontier level judgment. A legal analysis stays expensive not because it is hard but because checking it is hard, so you buy model quality as a substitute for a check you cannot build. This resolves a paradox nobody in the finance coverage has touched: coding became the frontier labs' biggest revenue line precisely because it is the most verifiable workload, and for the same reason it will be the first to commoditize out from under them.

This also tells you what to do when your workload has no compiler. Most non code work can be given a partial mechanical check: a schema with tight field constraints, domain invariants (totals sum, counts match, dates parse, references resolve), a golden set sampled into production traffic, and a human gate at the boundary where stakes concentrate. Our extraction tasks had no test suite, only schemas and invariants, and those checks alone caught every visible failure in the experiment. You do not need perfect verification to downgrade safely. You need verification that catches errors more cheaply than a 10x token premium does, and that bar is low.

The migration order of everything else follows from the same rule. Extraction and codegen first, templated documents and classification next, open ended analysis later, judgment work last, not because difficulty rises but because verification cost does.

The frontier premium buys you a quarter

Frontier models are a staging environment that the industry has been running as production. Fable 5 launched at exactly double Opus 4.8's price four days ago. The capability gap behind any frontier release now closes in months, not years, and cheaper tiers within the same families keep absorbing last quarter's capabilities at a tenth of the cost. What the premium buys is a lead measured in months, and that lead is worth everything for workloads you have not qualified yet and nothing for workloads you have.

Engineers already run this pattern everywhere else in the stack. Nobody ships nightly builds to production. You qualify a release, pin it, and run it until you have a reason to upgrade. The same operating model applies to models: the frontier tier is your canary channel, where you pay the premium to discover what new workloads become possible. The trailing tier is your LTS release, tested against your checks, pinned, cost efficient, running the daily load. A workload graduates from canary to LTS the day your verification layer says the cheap tier passes, and our one dollar experiment is what that graduation test looks like.

One reply on X asked why anyone would not route dynamically along the price and capability frontier by intelligence budget. The answer is that routing requires a confidence signal, and the confidence signal is the harness. You cannot send a task down market unless something tells you when the down market answer is wrong. Routing is not a model picker. It is a verification layer wearing a model picker as an interface.

Citadel's note lands one observation that deserves more credit than the finance coverage gave it: the durable productivity gains so far come from copilot patterns, not from agents running everything everywhere all at once. Their April companion note puts scale behind it. Reading 9,646 S&P 500 earnings call transcripts, they found executives framing AI as a complement to labor over a substitute by roughly 8 to 1, with 43 percent of firms calling it a complement and only 5 percent a substitute. Translate that out of macro language and it reads: the human is the cheapest verification layer ever deployed, which is exactly why copilot workflows are token efficient. Scope note for honesty: our experiment compared price tiers inside one family, not this year's frontier against last quarter's, so the vintage claim rests on the public lag data, not on our table.

What to do before the next release spikes the chart

Price the verification before you price the model. That is the entire decision rule. For each step in your pipeline, ask what a mechanical check on its output costs. If a schema, an invariant, a test suite, or a golden set can catch the failures, the step belongs on the cheap tier behind that check, and the frontier premium on that step is money spent on a problem you already solved. If the only thing that can check the output is frontier level judgment, then the premium is real insurance, and you should pay it knowingly rather than by default.

Then instrument the one metric this entire saga has been missing: spend per verified outcome. Not tokens, which Amazon's leaderboard proved are gameable. Not spend per task, which cannot tell productive work from echo. Dollars divided by outputs that passed a check you trust. Our experiment's headline numbers, 0.0019 against 0.0221, are that metric, and the gap between them is what your CFO actually wants to know.

Here is the prediction this framing makes, and you can hold the article to it. The expenditure index will keep sawtoothing. Every frontier release spikes it, because new capability arrives unverified and everyone rationally pays the insurance premium to explore it. Fable 5 landed on June 9 at twice Opus pricing; watch the index over the next weeks. Then harness maturity bleeds the index back down, as teams qualify the new workloads and pin them to cheaper tiers. The chart Wall Street reads as boom or bust is a qualification cycle, and its sawtooth is the sound of verification layers maturing, one workload at a time.

The bears watching for the rollover and the bulls waiting for the speed bump to pass are both watching the market's harness get built and calling it demand.
